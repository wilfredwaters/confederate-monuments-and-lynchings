Monuments and Lynchings

1890

About this map

Content warning: this map records racial terror killings.

It places two things on one timeline: where lynching victims were killed, and where Confederate monuments went up. Drag the slider or press Play. The chart below the map counts both, year by year: monuments above the line, lynchings below.

What the research says

Henderson and colleagues (2021, PNAS) compared counties in 11 Southern states. Counties with more lynchings had more Confederate monuments, even after accounting for population and other differences. The authors state the link is correlational: it does not show that monuments caused lynchings, or the reverse. Both may stem from the same local commitment to white supremacy.

The timing is part of the story. Most monuments went up between 1900 and 1920, in the years of Jim Crow laws and just after lynching peaked in the 1890s. A second, smaller wave came in the 1950s and 1960s, during the fight over civil rights. Watch the chart: dedications climb as lynching is still common.

How to read it

  • Everything up to the chosen year is shown. The last 10 years are bright; older items fade.
  • A white triangle with a blue outline is a monument removed or relocated, shown from its removal year. Most came down in 2017, after the Charlottesville rally, and in 2020, after the murder of George Floyd. Blue bars on the chart count removals per year.
  • Outside the 10 Tolnay–Beck states, victims are placed near the center of their county, a few miles apart, because the records give the county, not the exact spot. Inside them, DUSLR locates most victims to the town.
  • Click any mark for details. Accusations are those reported at the time; most were never tested in court.

Data

  • Lynchings, 38 states (1883–1941): Charles Seguin and David Rigby, "National Crimes: A New National Data Set of Lynchings in the United States, 1883 to 1941," Socius 2019. osf.io/kr8yc
  • Lynchings, 10 Southern states (1882–1930): Tolnay–Beck inventory, Center for Studies in Demography and Ecology, University of Washington. sites.uw.edu/lynching
  • Confederate symbols: Southern Poverty Law Center, Whose Heritage? Public Symbols of the Confederacy. Dedication year is missing for some symbols; those are left off the timeline ().
  • Place search: Nominatim, © OpenStreetMap contributors. Your search text is sent to it.
  • County centers: US Census Bureau 2023 Gazetteer. Base map: Esri World Light Gray Canvas (Esri, HERE, Garmin, © OpenStreetMap contributors).

Inspired by Monroe Work Today, which maps a larger lynching record (1835–1964). Its full data is not public, so it is not used here.

Limits

  • Every lynching count is an undercount: many killings were never reported.
  • The lynching data ends in 1941; monuments run to the present. After 1941 the chart shows monuments only.
  • Monument data covers symbols reported to and checked by SPLC; it misses some.
  • victims whose county is unknown are not mapped.
